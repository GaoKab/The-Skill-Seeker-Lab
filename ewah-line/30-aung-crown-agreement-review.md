# Aung Crown amended agreement — clause 2.2 review

**Document:** `agreements/AungCrown-NNN-amended-2026-09-21.pdf`, sent 7 and 21 Sept, read 25 Sept.
**Verdict: SAFE TO SIGN after two word-level changes.** Not a rewrite. Ask for both, hold firm on the first.

## What was asked for, and what came back

Requested on 5 Sept:

> "Any patterns, blocks, samples, or technical files developed from Party A's Confidential Information shall be the exclusive property of Party A and shall not be used for or disclosed to any other client."

What Yolanda drafted as **2.2 Ownership of Development Materials**, in two parts:

**Part one, the grant.** Patterns, blocks, samples or technical files "specifically custom-developed for Party A **solely based on** Party A's Confidential Information" are the exclusive property of Party A, and Party B shall not use or disclose "such Party-A-specific deliverables to any **third-party client**."

**Part two, the carve-out.** Party B's pre-existing IP stays Party B's: its generic base patterns, common grading rules, general manufacturing know-how and standard technical templates developed independently before receiving Party A's information. Party B may keep using its own generic, non-customised technologies and base patterns for other customers, **"provided that no Confidential Information or unique design features of Party A are incorporated therein."**

## The carve-out is fair. Accept it.

This is the part that looks alarming and is not. **No factory can sign away its own base blocks and grading rules** — doing so would stop it working for anyone else, and any factory that agreed to it would be either careless or lying. It is standard, it is correctly drafted, and the closing proviso does real work in EWAH's favour: they may reuse generic methods *only* where none of EWAH's confidential information or unique design features are in them.

Demanding its removal would be unreasonable and would probably end the relationship with the best-performing supplier in the project. Do not fight it.

## Change 1 — "solely based on" is the one that matters

This is a genuine escape hatch and it sits on the exact thing being protected.

A custom hat block is never created from nothing. It is made from the factory's base block **plus** the client's measurements and specifications. So a block developed for EWAH is, in plain fact, **not** "solely based on" EWAH's confidential information. Read strictly, part one may therefore protect almost nothing, while part two expressly reserves the base patterns the block was built from.

That is very unlikely to be a trap. It reads like a lawyer protecting the carve-out and overreaching by one word. But it is the difference between a clause that works and one that argues.

**Ask for:** "solely based on" → **"based on or derived from"**

Everything else in part one can stay. With that change the two halves fit together properly: their generic base block stays theirs, and the EWAH-specific block derived from it is EWAH's.

## Change 2 — "third-party client" leaves their own showroom open

Part one stops Party B disclosing the deliverables to a **third-party client**. It does not stop Party B using EWAH's hat in **its own** catalogue, website, showroom or trade-show stand as a capability sample. That is extremely common in hat and garment factories, and it is not a third-party client, so the clause as written permits it.

For a brand whose whole proposition is an original block, seeing that block on a factory's own capability page is a real harm.

**Ask for:** after "to any third-party client", add **"or in Party B's own products, samples, catalogue, website, showroom or trade show displays."**

## Change 3 — worth one sentence, not worth a fight

Clause 3 limits the obligations under Paragraph 2 to **three years**. Clause 2.2 sits inside Paragraph 2, so on a literal reading the ownership of EWAH's blocks could be argued to lapse in 2029. Ownership is a property right rather than a confidentiality obligation, so this is probably just untidy drafting.

**Ask for:** confirmation that the ownership in 2.2 is not time-limited by clause 3. If they will not add it, sign anyway — it is the weakest of the three points.

## Signing checklist, unchanged from the first review

- Party A signs as **"Gaofenngwe Kabubi, trading as EWAH"** — EWAH is not a registered company, and this is both honest and enforceable. The IP then sits with the founder personally and can be assigned into a company later.
- Party B's chop must read **深圳昂冠实业有限公司 / Shenzhen Angguan Industrial Co., Ltd.** A Chinese contract without the company chop is weak. A signature alone is not enough.
- The header still says "(Company Name)" for Party A. Fill in the personal-trading-name form above, not a company that does not exist.
- **Get the two-brand coverage onto the document**, not only in email. Yolanda has already agreed in writing that it covers both EWAH and the Botswana collection. One line naming both projects in the Project definition makes it survive a change of sales contact.
- Governing law is PRC with arbitration in the PRC or Singapore. Correct and enforceable for a Chinese supplier, as `28` sets out. Leave it.

## What this unlocks

One signed document releases three products across two brands: the EWAH bucket hat, the PULA beanie, the PULA bucket hat. Sample prices are already quoted at roughly $40 and $55.

## Revised version reviewed 3 Oct 2026 — VERDICT: SIGN IT

**Document:** `agreements/AungCrown-NNN-revised-2026-09-30.pdf`, supplied by the founder 3 Oct, four pages, bilingual.

**All three changes were made, and the one I told her to concede was granted too.** This is the best outcome available from this negotiation and there is nothing left worth asking for.

### Change 1 — granted, and improved on

Clause 2.2 now reads:

> "any custom block, pattern, grading or related work product specifically developed by Party B for Party A in connection with the Projects, **based on or derived from Party B's pre-existing base patterns, together with Party A's measurements, specifications and other project-specific information, shall belong to Party A.**"

They did not just swap the words. They moved the referent, and in doing so closed the loophole more thoroughly than the requested edit would have.

The old wording said deliverables developed "solely based on Party A's Confidential Information." The escape route in that sentence was obvious: a factory could say *this block is not solely based on your information, it is based on our base block plus your measurements, therefore it is ours.* That hybrid case is exactly how every custom block is actually built, so the old clause arguably owned nothing at all.

The new wording names that hybrid case explicitly and assigns it to Party A. The thing the money is being spent on is now the thing the clause covers.

**Residual gap, not worth raising.** As written, ownership attaches to work derived from their base pattern *together with* her information. A block built from her measurements with no base pattern involved is not literally described. That case does not occur in practice, and if it did the claim to it would be stronger, not weaker. Leave it.

### Change 2 — granted verbatim

> "Party B shall not disclose, provide, transfer, reproduce or otherwise make available any such Party A-owned custom block, pattern, grading or related work product to any third-party client **or in Party B's own products, samples, catalogue, website, showroom or trade show displays**, without Party A's prior written consent."

The exact words that were asked for. This was the change that stopped the factory from putting her silhouette in its own showroom while technically never showing it to a client.

### Change 3 — granted, and this one was not expected

> "For the avoidance of doubt, the ownership rights in this Section 2.2 are not subject to the three-year limitation in Section 3 and shall continue indefinitely, unless otherwise agreed by the parties in writing."

This was rated the weakest of the three and the instruction was to sign without it. They added it anyway. **Clause 3's three-year expiry now applies only to the confidentiality obligation, and the ownership of the blocks is permanent.** That is a materially better document than the one that was asked for.

### Fourth ask — in the body, not only the filename

Page one, before the recitals:

> 'The "Project" means and includes the EWAH brand work and the Botswana collection.'

With the Chinese mirror. Both brands are now defined inside the agreement, so one signature covers the EWAH bucket hat, the PULA beanie and the PULA bucket hat, and it survives a change of sales contact.

### The pre-existing-IP carve-out survived, correctly

Clause 2.2's second sentence keeps Party B's base patterns, grading rules, manufacturing methods, techniques and general know-how. That is the clause the first review said to accept and not fight, and it is still fair. It is now narrower in effect, because the first sentence pulls the custom hybrid work out of it.

### Two things worth noting that were not asked for

**Clause 2 bars IP registration.** *"Under any circumstances, Party B shall not file any intellectual property registration application which is originated from Confidential Information disclosed by Party A,"* naming patents, trademarks and copyright. This stops the single worst outcome in Chinese manufacturing, which is a supplier registering the trademark or design locally and then owning it.

**The Chinese text matches the English.** Clause 6 says English prevails in a dispute, which is the right way round, but a bilingual contract where the two halves say different things is a real risk and it was checked. The Chinese 2.2 mirrors the English on all three points: ownership of the hybrid custom work, the ban on own-product and trade-show use, and the exemption from the three-year limit.

### Governing law — unchanged, still correct

PRC law, arbitration in the PRC or Singapore. Enforceable against a Chinese supplier, which is the point. Leave it alone.
