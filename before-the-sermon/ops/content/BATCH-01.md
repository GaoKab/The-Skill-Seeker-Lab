## 01 · One Question card — Permission
**Pillar** Permission · **Format** cream text card · **CTA** save
**Card text:**
> "I don't know" is a complete answer.

**Caption:**
Most of us learned to read the Bible the way we learned to answer in class: quickly, and with the right answer.

So when a passage doesn't make sense, we assume the problem is us. We close the book, or we go and find someone to tell us what it meant.

There is a third option. You can write down that you don't know. That is not a failure of reading. It is the most honest thing a reader can say, and it is usually where the real reading starts.

Six questions. No right answers. The notebook is in the link.

## 02 · Sit With It carousel — Psalm 1
**Pillar** Sit With It · **Format** 5-slide carousel · **CTA** save + comment
**Slide 1:** *Psalm 1.* Read it twice. Once fast. Once slow.
**Slide 2:** *1. What does the text actually say?* — before any meaning: what is written, what is repeated, whose voice.
**Slide 3:** *2. What keeps pulling your attention?* — a word, a phrase, a turn you can't get past.
**Slide 4:** *4. What confused you, or sat uneasily?* — questions are welcome here. Nothing has to resolve.
**Slide 5:** What did you notice? There are no right answers in the comments either.

**Caption:**
Six verses. You could read Psalm 1 in forty seconds.

Try reading it twice instead, and answering three questions before you look anything up. Not what it means — what it *says*, what pulled at you, what didn't sit right.

This is the whole method. The notebook asks the same six questions of every passage, fifty-two times, until you stop needing the notebook.

Save this for Sunday morning.

## 03 · Reading, not interpreting — the refusal
**Pillar** Reading, not interpreting · **Format** text card · **CTA** share
**Card text:**
> This notebook does not tell you what it means.

**Caption:**
Every Bible resource I own explains the text to me. The study notes. The plan. The podcast. The sermon.

They are good. I am grateful for them. And I noticed I had never once read a passage without someone else's reading already in the room.

So this notebook does one thing the others don't: it withholds the explanation. Six questions, a passage, and space. The interpretation can come later — from your pastor, your group, your commentary. It will mean more when you have looked for yourself first.

A reading companion, not a replacement for sermons.

## 04 · Before Sunday — the ritual (Saturday)
**Pillar** Before Sunday · **Format** text card · **CTA** save
**Card text:**
> Before Sunday: read tomorrow's passage tonight.

**Caption:**
If your church posts the passage ahead of time, read it tonight. If it doesn't, ask — most will tell you.

Read it twice. Answer one question: *what keeps pulling your attention?* Write it down, even if it's one word.

Then tomorrow, listen to the sermon with a reading of your own already in your hands. Notice where the preacher goes somewhere you didn't. Notice where you went somewhere they didn't.

That gap is where you start to become a reader.

## 06 · One Question card — assumptions
**Pillar** Sit With It · **Format** text card · **CTA** comment
**Card text:**
> What are you bringing to it?

**Caption:**
Before you read a familiar passage, you already know what it means. Someone told you when you were nine. A sermon settled it. A verse on a mug.

Write that down first. Not to argue with it — to set it beside the page so you can see which words are the text's and which are yours.

This is question three of six. It is the one people skip, and the one that changes the most.

## 07 · Permission — the half-empty page
**Pillar** Permission · **Format** text card · **CTA** save
**Card text:**
> A half-empty page is not a failed reading.

**Caption:**
Some readings will take an evening. Some will take a season. Some you will open, read twice, write "I have no idea what this is doing here", and close.

That page counts. You looked. You said something true about what you found.

Silence was allowed from the start.
